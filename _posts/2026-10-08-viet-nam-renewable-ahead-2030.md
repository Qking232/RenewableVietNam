Viet Nam has become one of Southeast Asia's fastest-moving renewable energy
markets. Solar and wind capacity grew from almost nothing in 2018 to many
gigawatts within a few years, and the country now sits among the region's
leaders in installed solar. The next milestone on the national plan is 2030.

## Where things stand today

The build-out has been driven by strong sun, a long windy coastline, and a
power development plan that pushed hard on utility-scale solar. Three sources
carry most of the clean generation:

- **Solar** — concentrated in the sunny south and central provinces, from
  utility-scale farms to rooftop systems on homes and factories.
- **Wind** — onshore and near-shore projects along the coast, with offshore
  wind being prepared for the next phase.
- **Hydropower** — the long-standing backbone of the grid, now supplemented
  rather than expanded at scale.

> Viet Nam's challenge is no longer building renewable capacity — it is moving
> that electricity to where it is needed, when it is needed.

## What 2030 looks like

Between now and 2030 the story shifts from "how much can we build" to "how do
we integrate it". Four themes matter most:

1. **Grid and transmission** — new lines and substations to carry power from
   solar and wind zones to the big load centres, including Ho Chi Minh City
   and the industrial north.
2. **Storage** — batteries and pumped hydro to smooth the evening peak once
   solar stops producing.
3. **Offshore wind** — a large, still largely untapped resource that could
   supply baseload-like volumes if the regulatory framework settles.
4. **Direct power purchases** — allowing factories to buy clean power
   directly, which ties investment to real demand.

## Why it matters

A grid that runs on more solar, wind and water means cleaner air, less
exposure to imported fuel prices, and skilled work in provinces that need it.
It also means the hardest engineering work is still ahead: balancing a system
that is now much more variable than the one it replaced.

## Looking ahead

The 2030 target is ambitious but the direction is clear. The coming years will
be judged less on how many panels and turbines are installed, and more on
whether the grid, the storage and the market rules keep pace with them.
